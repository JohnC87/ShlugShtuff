Customer Portal — by AMCS

Designing an AI-Driven Customer Hub
From Static Portal to Intelligent Assistant

UX Research AI Design Enterprise Product

The AMCS Customer Portal is a next-generation platform designed to help customers sign up, manage container-based collection services, and handle billing for materials management in a faster, more intuitive way. I led the transformation of the experience from an outdated, low-adoption portal into an AI-driven product that rethinks how users interact with complex service systems.

What began as a UX redesign uncovered deeper challenges — manual onboarding processes and heavy reliance on support teams. The project evolved into a complete end-to-end reimagining, introducing an intelligent assistant that guides users, automates key steps, and enables instant access to services.

A next-generation, AI-powered self-service platform that transforms how customers sign up, manage, and interact with their material management services. Discovery
Understanding Why the Original Portal Failed

At first glance, the issue looked visual. Outdated screens, inconsistent layouts, unclear navigation. But after reviewing the full experience end to end, the real problem ran much deeper.

The product wasn't just difficult to use. It was difficult to trust.

I ran discovery workshops and brainstorming sessions with users and internal stakeholders to understand where frustration peaked, how people approached onboarding, and which parts of the portal they actively avoided. Stakeholder sessions surfaced the operational pain points and manual dependencies shaping the experience behind the scenes. Together they created a clear picture of both the user frustrations and the business challenges driving them.

What Users Actually Said
Real frustrations captured during sessions — not summaries, not interpretations.
As a new customer, I didn't know how to create an account.
I completed the form but nothing happened. I didn't know if it worked.
I couldn't tell which services were actually available at my location.
I gave up and called support. The portal just wasn't clear enough to trust.

From these sessions I was able to narrow the problems down to four core issues.

01. No clear entry point — users landed on a page with disconnected actions and no obvious path for new customers. There was no clear place to begin, and no clear way to create an account.

02. A flow that created more questions than answers — services were shown before location was confirmed, meaning users were choosing from options that might not even be available to them. The journey felt long, repetitive, and gave no sense of progress.

03. Manual handover breaking digital flow — completing the form wasn't the end of onboarding, it was the beginning of a wait. Here is what that actually looked like:

The most important moment in the experience was also the most broken. A digital product was still running on manual handover.

04. Trust didn't recover inside the portal — once users were eventually granted access, inconsistent layouts, disconnected sections, and the absence of a design system meant confidence continued to erode page by page.

After eight years in market, only two active customers remained.

This gave me a clear focus. Fix onboarding first. Then redesign the system around user independence, not operator dependency.

Decisions
01
Rebuilding Trust Through Structure
Phase 1

Before any AI could work, the foundations had to be solid. The original Customer Portal had no design system, no consistent patterns, and no clear navigation. Users didn't trust it because it didn't behave like a product that had been thought through.

The first phase wasn't glamorous. It was about fixing what should have been right from the start.

I built a full design system from the ground up. Components, spacing, hierarchy, interaction patterns — everything standardised so the product felt like one thing rather than a collection of disconnected screens. The onboarding journey was redesigned into a clear linear flow, the landing page simplified, and navigation restructured so users could immediately understand where to go and what to do next.

02
From Forms to Guided Decisions
Phase 2

The biggest bottleneck in the experience wasn't the interface. It was the decision-making the interface forced onto users.

Before they could sign up, users had to know their service type, their container size, their collection frequency. They were browsing options that might not even be available at their location. The system was asking for expertise users didn't have, then making them wait while an operator validated everything anyway.

Improving the forms wasn't going to fix this. The process itself was the problem.

The AI assistant changed the model entirely. Instead of users selecting from a catalogue, the assistant asks simple questions and makes the complex decisions in the background. The user confirms their company, their location is identified, services are recommended based on their business type, and pricing is generated in real time. No technical knowledge required. No waiting.

The goal was never to make onboarding feel like a chat. It was to make hard decisions feel effortless.

03
Designing AI Beyond Chat

The first version of the AI assistant was too close to a traditional chatbot. Long responses, no visible structure, and a blank input field that put all the pressure on the user to know what to say next.

Testing surfaced two things quickly. Users felt uncertain about progress — without any sense of where they were in the process, the conversation felt open-ended and risky, especially when it got to pricing and payment. Users also disengaged from heavy text responses. Reading through a paragraph to find the one piece of information they needed created fatigue fast.

Conflict A
A pure chat interface is flexible but creates no sense of structure or safety.
Conflict B
A traditional form is structured but kills the intelligence that makes AI useful.
First AI

The solution was a hybrid. AI handling the thinking, structured UI carrying the experience.

I introduced a stepper so users always knew where they were. An order summary panel updated in real time as decisions were made, so nothing felt hidden. Cards, maps, and comparison tables replaced long text responses wherever the information was complex. Quick action buttons reduced the need to type. And every step was editable, so users could adjust without restarting.

Detail
"I like how the order summary is there now — it keeps me on track."

The experience stopped feeling like talking to a machine and started feeling like being guided through something familiar. That was the shift.

04
Designing With AI, Not Just For AI

Phase 2 changed more than the product. It changed how I worked.

Using Claude Code, I connected our design system through MCPs and built a workflow where design, testing, and development moved at a pace that wasn't possible before. Instead of designing screens in Figma, handing them to developers, and waiting for a build to test, I was generating interactive coded UI directly, using design system rules, UX documentation, and component patterns as the foundation.

I also built UX agents trained on our own internal guidelines and principles. These agents generate first-pass UI that already respects our standards, which meant less time fixing basics and more time making real design decisions.

The output wasn't prototype-quality work that needed rebuilding. It was near-production code that developers could take and integrate directly. Design iteration time dropped by around 80%. A concept that previously took close to a week to get into a testable state could be validated in a single day.

05
From Portal to Product

Phase 1 made the experience usable. Phase 2 made the business model viable.

The original Customer Portal required operator time to onboard every single customer. That was a ceiling. Growth was limited not by demand but by the capacity of internal teams to manually process signups. A digital product that depended on human handover at its most critical moment was never going to scale.

The redesign stopped being a UX project somewhere along the way. It became a product strategy. One that changed what the platform could offer, who could use it, and how fast it could grow.

Customer Portal
Customer Portal
The AI assistant handles onboarding. But the product it leads into had to earn the same level of trust. Once users complete signup they land in a fully self-service Customer Portal. Every task that previously required a support call — modifying a service, downloading an invoice, updating payment details, checking a collection schedule — is handled directly inside the product. The same design system and interaction principles from onboarding carry through into every section, so nothing feels disconnected once users are inside.
Services

Users can view active services, request changes, and add new collections without speaking to anyone. The AI assistant extends into this flow too. Rather than navigating through menus, users can express what they need and the system responds accordingly.

Billing and Payments

Invoices, payment history, and upcoming charges are surfaced clearly with no ambiguity. A persistent payment summary mirrors the order summary from onboarding — same pattern, same logic, familiar from the moment users first used the product.

The CMS

Operators needed to be able to configure and brand the Customer Portal for their own customers without engineering involvement. So we built a CMS that gives them direct control over branding, content, and portal settings.

This meant the Customer Portal wasn't just a product for end users. It became a configurable tool that operators could deploy and maintain themselves, reducing their dependency on development resource and giving them genuine ownership of the experience they were offering their customers.

Closing

The Customer Portal is still being built. There are screens still being refined, edge cases still being designed for, and production integrations still being completed. That's the honest state of it.

But what exists already demonstrates something worth saying out loud. The AI assistant wasn't added to an existing product to make it feel modern. It was the solution to a structural problem that better design alone couldn't fix. The hybrid interaction model, the order summary panel, the design system that Claude Code generates against — none of it happened by following a standard UX process. It happened by being willing to change how we worked, not just what we made.

Phase 1 gave users a product they could actually use. Phase 2 gave them a product that does the hard work for them. The difference between those two things is bigger than it sounds.

This project is still running. But it has already changed what I think good design work looks like, and what I think a designer's job actually is.
