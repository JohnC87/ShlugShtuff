The Research Trip
Going to Amsterdam

Before designing anything, I went to meet the people who would use it.

Meerlanden, a waste management company operating across all of Amsterdam, were using a legacy version of AMCS Mobile but hadn't committed to the new platform. The connection between their system and the app was incomplete, causing lags and unreliable data. They were considering walking away from the product entirely.

I proposed spending a week with their team. Not to sell, but to understand.

Meerlanden signed up to both AMCS Mobile and the full AMCS platform the following year. The research trip was one of the reasons.
Outcome — Amsterdam, 2024

The week started at the depot each morning. Dispatchers would arrive and manually assign routes to drivers, a process that immediately sparked an idea. Drivers would gather for a morning debrief before heading to their trucks, signing in with credentials, then signing the vehicle in separately. It was slow. Some drivers had started leaving random accounts permanently signed into trucks just to avoid the friction. A product that gets worked around like that has already failed its users.

I joined three truck trips across different lines of business: municipal household collections, underground bin collections requiring specialist arm trucks, and commercial skip and container collections. Each operated completely differently. Each had its own frustrations that the app wasn't designed for.

I interviewed the project manager, two dispatch managers covering municipal and commercial operations, and two drivers. I also spent time in the dispatch room watching how the team managed live operations and investigated the depot scales where weights were being recorded by pen and paper rather than digitally.

By the end of the week I had a clear picture of what the next generation of this product needed to do. And Meerlanden had a clear picture of what working with AMCS could look like when someone actually listened.

The following year they signed up to both the mobile app and the full AMCS platform. The research trip was one of the reasons.

Discovery
What I Found in the Trucks

The legacy app had been built around a model that made sense on paper but broke down in the field. Each house was a separate collection job. Drivers were expected to work through them one by one, completing each individually before moving to the next.

In the dense streets of Amsterdam, this was impossible. Bins were left out with no way to tell which belonged to which house. Helpers on the ground would grab every bin they could reach while the truck kept moving. There was no time to scan or confirm individual addresses. So drivers did what made sense to them. They completed every job at the end of a street regardless of what was actually collected, and billed accordingly. The app was being used in a way it was never intended because the way it was intended didn't work.

This wasn't a driver problem. It was a model problem.

01
The house number model failed in high density areas
Individual job completion made no operational sense when bins couldn't be matched to addresses. Drivers needed to complete streets, not houses.
02
Team coordination was happening on personal phones
Drivers relied on text messages and personal calls to ask teammates to cover jobs when trucks were full or running behind. None of this was reflected in the system. It created confusion, gaps in records, and meant drivers were managing two devices while operating heavy machinery.
03
Sign-in was slow enough that people were cheating it
Credentials plus separate vehicle sign-in was taking long enough that drivers left accounts permanently signed in. The security and accountability the sign-in was meant to provide was being bypassed entirely.
04
Commercial collections couldn't handle container density
Zoomed out map views showed overlapping pins with no way to understand how many containers were at a location or how to prioritise them.
05
The app pulled attention away from the operation
Completing a job required navigating away from the map. Drivers hated it and stopped using it. They pressed complete, went back to the map, and the nuance the system needed was lost.
06
Time and events on route were invisible
Fuel stops, breaks, full truck returns to depot. None of this was tracked. Dispatch had no visibility into what was actually happening on a route.

After years of the legacy product, experienced drivers had developed their own systems to work around its limitations. The next generation needed to be built around how the work actually happened, not how it was supposed to happen.

Define
The Premise

If the app understood the operation rather than imposing a model onto it, drivers would spend less time looking at a screen and more time doing their job.

The core design principle for Next Gen Mobile was simple. Every interaction should be as short as possible, as automatic as possible, and as invisible as possible. The app should respond to what is happening, not wait for a driver to tell it.

What we were designing for:

Principle 01
Reduced screen time
Drivers are operating heavy vehicles in busy urban environments. Every second spent looking at a tablet is a second not watching the road. The goal was to design an app that only needed attention when something genuinely required a decision.
Principle 02
Street-level intelligence
The app needed to understand how municipal collection actually works — by street not by house — and build completion logic around that reality.
Principle 03
Operational continuity
Events like fuel stops, full trucks, and route handovers needed to flow through the app rather than disappear into personal phones and end-of-day verbal reports.
Decisions
01
Rethinking Collection from House to Street

The shift from house number completion to street completion was the most significant design decision in the project. It came directly from sitting in the truck.

Rather than asking a driver to confirm individual addresses, the app uses GPS to detect when a truck enters a street and begins tracking completion automatically. A street appears blue before the truck arrives, turns amber when the truck is detected on it, and completes to green when 75% of the street has been covered. The 75% threshold exists because GPS accuracy cannot be trusted to be perfect. Designing for the real world rather than the ideal one.

Streets were grouped into segments that planners could configure, combining related streets into logical units that matched how routes were actually structured. Turning on navigation shows the optimal route through all incomplete segments as a white line overlaid on the map.

Drivers who know their routes noticed things about certain streets that no GPS would ever capture. Some roads are too narrow for a large truck to turn around in. Others require a specific approach to avoid getting stuck. Rather than losing that knowledge when an experienced driver leaves, I designed the ability for dispatch or senior drivers to leave notes on specific streets.

When a truck nears capacity, a pop-up prompts the driver to navigate to the nearest disposal site. The route pauses so streets don't accidentally complete while the truck is away. The same logic applies to breaks and fuel stops. All of this generates a time record that dispatch can review.

02
Replacing Personal Phones with In-App Team Management

In every truck I sat in, drivers had two devices. The AMCS tablet for the official record and their personal phone for everything else — coordinating with teammates, asking someone to cover a segment, letting dispatch know a truck was full. None of it made it into the system.

I designed a team management layer that gave drivers visibility of their teammates on the map and allowed work to be shared directly through the app. A team leader can see every member's progress, estimated time remaining, and current location in real time. When a driver needs to hand off work, they select a teammate from the sharing screen. The app shows their progress, how close they are, and how much capacity they have.

Roles were built into the system deliberately. Only team leaders can allocate and share work between members. Drivers can see their team and communicate but cannot unilaterally reassign jobs. This matched the hierarchy that already existed in the depot and gave dispatch the oversight they needed without rebuilding how the team operated.

03
Eliminating the Sign-In Friction

Drivers leaving random accounts signed into trucks permanently was a symptom of a sign-in process that asked too much at the wrong moment.

Manual fallback options exist throughout for new drivers who haven't registered biometrics yet, or where the QR code is damaged or missing. The quick routes are the default. The manual options are always there.

04
Making Commercial Collections Navigable

Containers at nearby locations would overlap on the map, making it impossible to see how many were present or where exactly they were. The legacy app showed individual pins regardless of zoom level, which became an unusable mass of icons when zoomed out.

A driver could glance at the map from a distance and immediately understand where the work was concentrated, plan their approach before getting into the detail, and only encounter individual containers when they were close enough for it to matter.

Handling exceptions — containers that couldn't be accessed, weren't on site, or had contamination issues — was a painful process in the legacy app. I redesigned the exception flow to be as fast as possible: quick-select rejection reasons, photo capture built in, minimal taps to log and move on.

05
Designing Around the Driver, Not the System

The final layer of decisions wasn't about any single feature. It was about what the experience felt like as a whole.

Drivers noticed that bright screens were uncomfortable at night and on dark routes. Dark mode designed as a genuine interface, not just an inverted colour scheme. The map view could be switched between top-down, isometric, and driving view. Experienced drivers navigating familiar routes preferred top-down. Drivers new to a route preferred the driving view that followed their direction of travel.

Everything related to directions, segments, and route progress stayed on the map. Nothing required navigating away to a separate screen. Notifications, capacity warnings, team communications, break prompts all appeared as overlays or pop-ups on the map surface.

Settings gave each driver control over their own experience: auto-complete thresholds based on GPS distance, which icons to show on the map, notification preferences, whether other drivers were visible. Experienced drivers could tune everything. New drivers could leave the defaults and everything would still work.

Minimum presses. Maximum awareness. Keep the driver's attention where it needs to be.

Closing

Next Gen Mobile is still in development. There are lines of business still to be designed for, edge cases still to resolve, and features still to be refined before production release.

But the project already delivered something beyond the product itself. A company that was considering abandoning the legacy app and the platform behind it chose instead to sign up to both. The research trip that made that happen wasn't in the original brief. It was a decision to go and understand the work properly before designing anything.

The best design decisions in this project weren't made at a desk. They were made at 6am in a depot car park, watching drivers figure out which account had been left signed into the truck, and realising the app had been asking the wrong questions all along.
